Without strong fundamentals, learners often lack the knowledge of key questions to ask of their own work. For instance, many computer science programs have deemphasised fundamental topics like concurrency, operating systems and networking in favour of exposing students to more "real world" tech stacks. Arguably, this leads engineers to struggle to even conceive of what performance bottlenecks are in their perview (Casey Muratori puts it well [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jHLbL1Eg4gM&t=6820s)). The right education here is "unrealistic" in the sense that engineers rarely work directly at those levels.

I really had to think about this when delivering lectures on Turing machines in a "models of computation" subject. Specifically, I wanted to convey to students why the subject was relevant. Moreover, I wanted to do this without concocting nebulous real world applications, and also appreciating that only some of the students would be as fascinated by the Church-Turing Thesis and undecidability results as I am. So really what it came down to was the argument that **a good software engineer should have the kind of mind that can program at the level of a Turing Machine**, and moreover **a computer science degree should show you have that kind of mind.**

More pithily, you have your whole life to learn the next JavaScript framework, but fundamentals are best developed in one's formative years.

On a related note, the drive to frontload industry trends via universities makes me think we should actually be suspicious of educational institutions being held responsible for _all_ knowledge and opinions, i.e., some things should be decided on the ground. For instance, I was listening to an [Interview of divorce attorney James Sexton](https://youtu.be/lWy0telFEnY?t=1655), where James laments that school taught him lots about dividing fractions, but nothing on conflict resolution. I thought he meant well, but is it really a school's job to set parameters and opinions on communication among lovers? Is that not the job of families and communities? Indeed, those sorts of sentiments are among the many calls for "the realistic" in education going too far.
