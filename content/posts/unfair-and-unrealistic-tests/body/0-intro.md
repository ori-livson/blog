The following are understandable criticisms of schooling and testing (and not just because I have held them all at some point).

1. Timed exams are unfair because the conditions are unrealistic.
2. Closed-book exams are especially unrealistic.
3. Curricula should emphasise more "real world content".

These criticisms are commonly levied against high schools, universities, technical interviews for jobs, and many other domains. Their resolution typically involves increasing the use of assignments (to better reflect real world scenarios) and in turn requires AI to be embraced (because its use can barely be regulated in assignments otherwise).

The purpose of this post is then to explore whether making education more realistic in the above sense is prudent.

In the educational context I will argue that it is not because core knowledge ought to be learned properly, and that developing one's capacity for independence, patience, careful reading and memorisation matters now more than ever.

Moreover, the relationship of learning the above to learning from projects / on the jobs is like how exercise relates to playing a sport. **Trying to learn with an exercised mind is especially difficult, and exercise is often by definition best done in unrealistic settings.**

In the context of technical interviews, a subtly related argument is that **we should also not shy away from trivia.** Trivia being peripheral, perhaps strange or edge-case questions, whose answers tend to be learned through experience, interest, getting one's hands dirty, deep rather than surface level reading, etc. In other words, good testing in this context should require some serendipity but not too much for good candidates.

Of course there are a lot of wrong ways to do old-fashioned education and testing, but I hope to demonstrate there is actually a lot to be gained by taking a step back and figuring out how to educate and test effectively, rather than clumsily indulging AI inevitabilism.
